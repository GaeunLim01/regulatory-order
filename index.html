AI GOVERNANCE × REGULATORY ORDER

Regulation has to decide what it is even holding.

When a new technology becomes a matter of national security, I read recent cases for where that grip holds and where it slips.

Five cases, meant as a sample of the pattern below, not a running list of every case that fits it. They sit in the U.S. and the EU because that is where this kind of friction has been loudest, not because the pattern only works there.

How I read a case

Most accounts of a case stop at the outcome — a ban, a ruling, a seizure. Reading past that leaves a few questions standing, whatever the case. Not stages of a shared model, just what's left once the outcome stops being the interesting part.

Who designed the categories this rests on, and who only gets to interpret them?

Every case here rests on some body of categories — export, reexport, transfer; a designation meant for one kind of vendor; a transparency duty scoped to one market. Someone wrote those categories, usually a legislature, usually in general terms. A different body — an agency, a regulator, a company reading its own obligations — only gets to rule on whether a new situation fits inside them. That split, and which side is actually holding the pen when a new technology shows up, is worth checking first.

Where did that split leave a gap new enough that nobody had designed for it?

New technology tends to sit outside whatever was designed, not because anyone missed it, but because designing for something before it exists isn't really possible. A chip reachable without moving. A model whose weights might need policing on their own. An access request no category was written for. The interesting gaps are the ones neither the designer nor the interpreter actually built for.

What did each actor do into that gap — and where does what they meant to do come apart from what actually happened, and who ends up carrying the difference?

This is where a case actually lives. Once a gap exists, actors move into it: an agency short on statutory authority races ahead of the legislature anyway; a company picks the cheaper global fix over the narrower legal one; an individual routes a shipment through the seam between two definitions. Almost none of them land the outcome they were aiming for. The rule built to hold a company catches an employee instead. A tool built to flag foreign ownership gets pointed at a domestic policy dispute. A label meant for one market becomes the default everywhere because matching it exactly costs more than exceeding it. Somebody, every time, ends up holding a difference they didn't sign up for.
