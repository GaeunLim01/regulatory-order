Framework v3

How regulatory order reaches actors

Three-module structure, with quantitative fields on Downstream.

Module 1 · Instability

Why is the regulated object unstable under the novelty of AI as a technology?

Module 2 · Formation History

How did today's regulatory boundary come to take its current shape?

Modules 1 and 2 answer genuinely independent questions. A given case can be classified on the Instability axis and the Formation-History axis at the same time, without one determining the other.

Module 3 · Downstream

Once an order exists — regardless of whether it arose through an Instability-type collision or a Formation-History-type proactive amendment — how does it reach actors, and how are consequences distributed? Module 3 is different in kind: it does not classify cases, it describes a common downstream process that applies once an order exists, no matter which module produced it.

This framework maps how regulatory order forms and reaches actors across AI-related value chains: semiconductors, computing, cloud, data centers. It did not originate as a single top-down taxonomy. It grew out of two separate lines of inquiry, plus a third layer describing what happens once order is in place. Earlier drafts tried to nest all of this under one branching tree with Event Type at the top, which produced recurring friction: items that belonged to one inquiry kept showing up as awkward children of the other. This version keeps the three modules explicitly independent.

Each case in the case library carries two independent classification tags, Module 1 and Module 2, and, where documented, a set of Module 3 observations.

Scope

This framework applies to regulatory orders that national or international authorities currently form or operate through documented rules, formal classifications, and administratively recognizable decisions. Tracing is confined to the documentary record: published rules, official gazettes, consultation records, court filings, and industry comments. Exchanges that never entered that record are out of reach; this does not mean such paths don't exist, only that this method cannot see them.

Module 1

Instability

Under the novelty of AI as a technology, why is the regulated object itself unstable? Built from actual incidents — chip-smuggling cases, Anthropic–U.S. government tension — read for what made the object hard to pin down.

1.1 What kind of thing is being regulated

TypeDefinitionWhat stays stable
Physical goodsIdentity is comparatively fixed (chips, steel); movement is the key event.Object identity; jurisdiction and definitional authority stay separable.
Access & dataAccess, use, and transmission are the key events.Nothing stays stable — identity, jurisdiction, and definitional authority are entangled.
Capabilityphysical + access combinedPhysical and informational layers are inseparably combined; this is usually where “capability” sits.Each layer is legible on its own, but the combined regulatory reading splits — often where events begin.
RelationalThe tie between parties is itself the regulated object (contracts, equity stakes, supply agreements, access arrangements).Parties are generally identifiable; the scope of the tie and its downstream inheritance rarely settle.

1.2 Why the collision happened

Two branches, with an overlapping region. These are a typology of collision, not properties of a single object — a value emerges only when there are two colliding sides.

ItemBranchWhat it points to
Cross-border authority clashAuthority-drivenActors want different things from the object and assert the authority to make their purpose prevail.
Speed mismatch (law vs. tech)StructuralPersists even when purposes align, because procedure and the object run on different cycles.
Both at once (compounded)OverlapA structural gap widens further because purposes also diverge.

In the semiconductor corpus, one side of the collision was nearly always fixed: the speed-mismatch item was consistently “legislative cycle vs. pace of technical change,” and the authority-clash item was consistently “U.S. jurisdiction vs. offshore actors.” Because that side never varied, only the item name was recorded. In other domains, both colliding sides should be written out explicitly.

Module 2

Formation History

How did today's regulatory boundary come to take its current shape? Built by tracing the documentary record backward — for example, U.S. BIS computing and semiconductor export-control rules from 2009 through the 2026 Remote Access Security Act — to find structural features in how the boundary was set or reset.

2.1 How the rule changed

This is not a flat three-way classification. It is a two-step branch, and the earlier three-column layout obscured that hierarchy.

Step 1 — Is there a prior state to compare against?
  • No → New rule from scratch (Enactment) Obligations arise whole; no accumulated cases yet, so Step 2 and the Formation Trace questions below do not apply.
  • Yes → go to Step 2
Step 2 — Did actors collide with one another directly?
  • Yes → Reactive (Conflict)
  • No → Proactive (Amendment) No direct collision, but the amendment creates a burden for other actors later.

Earlier drafts listed Enactment / Amendment / Conflict as three parallel columns, which made Enactment's blank cells (no collision points, no timeline markers) look like missing data. They are not missing — Enactment exits the branch at Step 1, before the questions that produce those cells even apply.

2.2 Timeline of how the rule got here

Conflict and Amendment cases are both described through a timeline traced backward from the present. The method is the same; what gets marked on the timeline differs by type.

Event TypeWhat gets marked on the timeline
Reactive (Conflict)Points where actors collided directly.
Proactive (Amendment)The path along which burden arises after the amendment.
New rule from scratch (Enactment)Not applicable — no prior timeline to trace.

The point at which the order began operating is also marked on this timeline. Example corpus for this module: U.S. BIS computing/semiconductor export-control rules, 2009 → 2026 Remote Access Security Act.

Module 3

Downstream

Once an order exists, how does it reach actors and how are its consequences distributed? This applies uniformly regardless of the Module 1 or Module 2 classification of the case — a Conflict-born order and an Amendment-born order are transmitted, distributed, and responded to through the same mechanisms.

3.1 How the rule actually reaches you

Order does not reach actors through legal text alone — it travels through classifications, deadlines, contracts, documentation systems, dependencies, access decisions, and enforcement signals.

The real operative deadlinedeadlinesThe operative deadline is often a preparation gate earlier than the formal legal date.Which clock actually governs?
Passed down through contractscontractual relationsPrivate clauses carry data demands, cost pass-through, and replacement obligations past the legal addressee.How does the rule travel beyond the named party?
What you have to prove, and howdocumentation systemsThe system demanding evidence is where exposure is felt.What must be produced, and who can produce it?
Exposure depends on your spot in the chainsupply-chain dependenceThe same order arrives differently depending on upstream or downstream position.Where in the chain does the burden land?

3.2 Who ends up holding the bag

Position and leverage. Source and Bearer are relational positions, not fixed identities — the same actor can be a Source for one order and a Bearer for another.

TermKindDefinition
SourcegeneratorPositionThe actor producing the underlying capability, asset, demand, or regulatory trigger around which order forms.
BearerabsorberPositionThe actor standing where the order actually produces consequences, often carrying nominal responsibility or immediate exposure.
Bargaining powerleverageCapacityThe ability to contest, deflect, absorb, or pass on a burden. Frequently shapes the final distribution more than formal position does.
MechanismMeaning
Named party ≠ paying partyburden displacementThe party a rule formally names is not necessarily the party that ultimately absorbs the cost or risk.
Costs roll downhilldownstream propagationConsequences travel along operational dependencies; actors with little power over the original dispute inherit the disruption, cost, and legal exposure.
Enforcement makes it heavierenforcement amplificationInvestigations, penalties, and comparable cases intensify the burden attached to an existing order without any change to the legal text. Distinct from Transmission's “enforcement signals” row: that concerns how order reaches actors; this concerns how weight already in place gets redistributed.

When several orders operate at once (can be set aside in single-case diagnosis): a single actor may face several orders on different timetables simultaneously, and exposures that are each manageable in isolation can erode effective leverage once they accumulate.

3.3 How firms reshape the field in response

Firms restructure supply chains, redesign products, rewrite contracts, relocate jurisdictions, lobby, and create new intermediaries. These responses change what is visible, who appears responsible, and where strategic risk sits. The altered field feeds into the next round of Module 2 — this is feedback the actors generate themselves.

External shocks that force change (Contextual Amplification): geopolitical and macroeconomic conditions — war, sanctions realignment, currency shocks, political turnover, financial crisis — sit in the background at low intensity and can raise the stakes attached to an order already in place. Past a threshold, they can trigger re-formation directly. Unlike firms' own adaptation, this route comes from outside the field the order governs.

Quantifiable fields for 3.3
FieldUnitWhat it measures
Time-to-responsedaysFrom the order taking effect to the first documented firm response.
Time-to-normalizationdaysFrom first response to restored or steady-state operation, where applicable.
Contextual amplification eventscountNumber of external shocks (war, sanctions, political turnover, etc.) documented as coinciding with this re-formation, if any.

These numeric fields apply only to Module 3. Modules 1 and 2 remain purely qualitative — they classify what kind of thing is happening, not how much of it. Forcing a score onto a classification judgment (e.g. rating “how much of a Jurisdiction Contest” a case is) manufactures false precision rather than adding information.

Worked example — chip-smuggling case: Object Type = Capability (physical + access combined); Collision source = Cross-border authority clash; Event Type = Reactive (Conflict). These are two independent tags (Module 1 + Module 2) on the same case, not a parent–child relationship.
