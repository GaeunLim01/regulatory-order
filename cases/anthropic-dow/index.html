Case narrative

Anthropic maintained restrictions on mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons. The Department of Defense sought contractual language allowing "all lawful purposes." When the company refused, the government deployed supply-chain risk designation and agency-wide cessation orders. The episode exposed a contest over where the use-boundary should be drawn and the limits of available instruments.

Department of Defense remains the department's legal name. Executive Order 14347 (September 2025) authorized "Department of War" as a secondary title for non-statutory communications, the usage reflected in this case's short name (DoW).

01

Ordering

Open question

How is the jurisdictional or use boundary of this order drawn, and where is it tested, strained, or reconfigured?

The order operates through procurement contracts, security requirements for classified systems, and residual executive authorities (supply-chain risk designation, Defense Production Act). The contested boundary is permissible downstream use: Anthropic excluded mass surveillance of Americans and fully autonomous lethal systems; the government sought a boundary defined only by "lawful purposes."

02

Manifestation

Open question

Through what instruments does the order operate, and where do operational limits or gaps between design and value-chain realities appear?

Instruments deployed: contractual use restrictions, supply-chain risk designation, presidential directive to cease agency use, threat of Defense Production Act compulsion, and subsequent litigation.

When negotiation failed, the government shifted to coercive instruments. The episode exposed the still-fluid character of the national-security order for frontier models: available tools are powerful yet relatively blunt, and they generated legal challenge and operational friction (the phase-out of an already-deployed model).

03

Distribution

Open question

How do informational requirements and responsibility burdens created by this order move across actors?

Authority to define the use-boundary was claimed by both the company (through safety and acceptable-use policies) and the government (through national-security and procurement authorities). The supply-chain risk designation aimed to extend pressure beyond direct contracts to the wider contractor ecosystem. Detailed knowledge of contemplated end uses sat primarily with government customers; responsibility for downstream misuse risk remained in contention.

Cross-cutting observation

This case centers on a boundary conflict over downstream use rather than physical export or remote access. Technological capability created pressure for a clearer boundary; when private and public actors drew that boundary differently, strong but imperfect instruments were deployed; the limits of those instruments became visible.
