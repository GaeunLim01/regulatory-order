Case narrative

Since a 2009 BIS Advisory, the provision of computational capacity itself was treated as outside the EAR definition of an export. As advanced chips became accessible remotely via cloud and IaaS, that interpretive boundary left a growing gap. The Remote Access Security Act seeks to bring certain forms of remote access within BIS authority.

01

Ordering

Open question

How is the jurisdictional or use boundary of this order drawn, and where is it tested, strained, or reconfigured?

The existing EAR order, as interpreted since 2009, focused on physical movement or transmission of commodities, software, or technology—not on remote use of controlled hardware already located abroad. RASA seeks to redraw that boundary by amending the Export Control Reform Act to authorize BIS control over defined “remote access” when national-security risk is determined.

02

Manifestation

Open question

Through what instruments does the order operate, and where do operational limits or gaps between design and value-chain realities appear?

Earlier instruments: 2009 and subsequent BIS Advisories; EAR classification, licensing, knowledge standards, and FDPR rules focused on physical items. Controlled chips could sit in data centers outside China and still be accessed remotely for model training. The formal order controlled the chips as items but did not clearly control remote use of those chips.

This is a gap between regulatory design (item-based export controls + the 2009 interpretive boundary) and value-chain realities (cloud/IaaS delivery of compute). RASA operationalizes a new instrument: statutory authority over remote access under defined conditions.

03

Distribution

Open question

How do informational requirements and responsibility burdens created by this order move across actors?

Under the pre-RASA order, the 2009 boundary transmitted limited compliance obligations to pure IaaS providers compared with physical exporters. If enacted, RASA would redistribute burden: cloud and infrastructure providers facilitating remote access to controlled items could face new licensing, due-diligence, and potential liability obligations. Information about end-users of remote compute would become a more central compliance demand.

Cross-cutting observation

A technological shift (remote, large-scale use of controlled compute) exposed a category mismatch with the existing EAR order. The long-standing interpretive boundary transmitted a limited compliance obligation into the value chain. The resulting control failure became visible, prompting an attempt at statutory reconfiguration of the jurisdictional boundary itself.
