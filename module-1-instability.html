This module is built from actual incidents, read for what made the regulated object hard to pin down. It classifies the object and the collision, not the regulatory action that followed — that is Module 2's question, and the two are answered separately.

1.1

What kind of thing is being regulated

Object Type. Exactly one value per case.

  • Physical goodsphysical_goods

    Identity is comparatively fixed — chips, steel. Movement is the key event.

    What stays stable: object identity; jurisdiction and definitional authority stay separable.
  • Access & dataaccess_data

    Access, use, and transmission are the key events.

    What stays stable: nothing. Identity, jurisdiction, and definitional authority are entangled.
  • Capabilitycapability

    Physical and informational layers are inseparably combined. This is usually where “capability” sits.

    What stays stable: each layer is legible on its own, but the combined regulatory reading splits — often where events begin.
  • Relationalrelational

    The tie between parties is itself the regulated object: contracts, equity stakes, supply agreements, access arrangements.

    What stays stable: parties are generally identifiable; the scope of the tie and its downstream inheritance rarely settle.
1.2

Why the collision happened

Collision Typology. Two branches with an overlapping region. These are a typology of collision, not properties of a single object — a value emerges only when there are two colliding sides.

  • Cross-border authority clashauthority_clash · authority-driven

    Actors want different things from the object and assert the authority to make their purpose prevail.

  • Speed mismatch, law vs. techspeed_mismatch · structural

    Persists even when purposes align, because procedure and the object run on different cycles.

  • Both at oncecompounded · overlap

    A structural gap widens further because purposes also diverge.

Corpus limitation · record both sides

In the semiconductor corpus, one side of the collision was nearly always fixed. The speed-mismatch item was consistently “legislative cycle vs. pace of technical change,” and the authority-clash item was consistently “U.S. jurisdiction vs. offshore actors.” Because that side never varied, only the item name was recorded.

That is a property of that corpus, not of the framework. Once cases outside U.S. export control enter the library, the fixed side stops being fixed: an EU or Korean order will not have “U.S. jurisdiction” standing on one side of every authority clash. Both colliding sides should be written out explicitly.

1.3

What firms actually feel on the ground

Firm-Level Traces. Zero or more per case. These track instability at the level of individual firm experience, not the type of regulatory action that produced it — they can appear whether a case is later tagged Conflict or Amendment on the Formation-History axis.

  • Who's covered keeps shiftingboundary_drift

    The core legal text looks stable, yet who falls under the rule keeps shifting.

  • Different actors, different definitionsdefinitional_contest

    Several actors apply different working definitions to the same object at once.

  • Contract date vs. actual use don't line upcompeting_clocks

    Fixed-cycle processes — contract dates, legal effective dates — run alongside continuous, cumulative processes such as ongoing access, use, and volume.

  • The threshold keeps getting resetrecurring_reset

    A separate fixed-cycle process — annual review, delegated legislation — keeps resetting the threshold itself.

No scoring on this axis

Module 1 carries no numeric fields, and none may be added. Rating how much of a collision a case represents manufactures false precision rather than adding information. The quantified part of the template lives entirely in Module 3.
